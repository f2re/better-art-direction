# Composition and direction selection

Use this reference when defining or comparing art-direction candidates. Choose by product fit, not novelty for its own sake.

## Contents

- Candidate generation
- Scoring model
- Structural strategies
- Signature selection
- Density and rhythm
- Responsive translation
- Rejection rules

## Candidate generation

Generate two or three meaningfully different candidates before implementation. Change the information structure or dominant relationship, not merely the palette.

For each candidate, state:

- The product fact it expresses.
- The primary user path it privileges.
- The dominant spatial relationship.
- The proposed signature.
- The content or data required to make it credible.
- The implementation cost and risk.
- The reason it is not interchangeable with an unrelated product.

Do not expose weak brainstorming unless the user asks for alternatives. Select and present the strongest direction with concise rationale.

## Scoring model

Score each candidate from 1 to 5:

| Axis | 1 | 3 | 5 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Product fit | Could fit almost anything | Uses some domain cues | Structure emerges from the real workflow or subject |
| Clarity | Obscures the task | Understandable with effort | Primary job and next action are immediate |
| Distinctiveness | Common template | Some recognizable choices | Memorable without relying on fashion effects |
| Durability | Trend-dependent | Viable with maintenance | Remains coherent as content and features grow |
| Accessibility | Requires remediation | Mostly usable | Semantics and interaction are integral |
| Implementation cost | Disruptive or fragile | Moderate | Proportionate to value and existing stack |

Reject any candidate with product fit or clarity below 3. Prefer the candidate with the best combined fit, clarity, and durability; distinctiveness is not allowed to compensate for confusion.

## Structural strategies

These are starting structures, not templates. Adapt them to the information.

### Workbench

A stable command or navigation region surrounds a primary work surface. Appropriate for operational, technical, and creative tools.

Use when:

- The user repeatedly manipulates one central artifact.
- Context and tools must remain available.
- Efficiency matters more than narrative sequence.

Potential signature:

- A domain-specific canvas, document map, synchronized inspector, or command surface.

### Evidence-first

Open with the product artifact, live state, or output rather than a marketing headline. Appropriate when the product can demonstrate value directly.

Use when:

- The evidence is understandable without a long explanation.
- A screenshot, map, generated document, simulation, or interactive result carries the proposition.

Potential signature:

- A live demonstration, annotated artifact, or before-and-after transformation.

### Guided sequence

A linear flow reveals only the information needed at each stage. Appropriate for checkout, onboarding, configuration, and high-stakes forms.

Use when:

- Order matters.
- Validation and recovery are central.
- Users benefit from clear completion state.

Potential signature:

- A precise progress model tied to real decisions rather than decorative step numbers.

### Comparative field

Several options, periods, scenarios, or entities share a common visual coordinate system. Appropriate for analytics, commerce, planning, and decision support.

Use when:

- Comparison is the primary task.
- Isolated cards would hide relationships.

Potential signature:

- Coordinated scales, synchronized focus, a comparison rail, or a spatial matrix.

### Editorial argument

The page follows an evidence-based narrative with controlled changes in scale and pace. Appropriate for marketing, research, and editorial surfaces.

Use when:

- The product needs persuasion or explanation.
- The order of evidence matters.

Potential signature:

- A subject-specific typographic construction, annotated source material, or one orchestrated media moment.

### Spatial index

Navigation and content form a visible map, taxonomy, timeline, or topology. Appropriate for archives, document systems, GIS, courseware, and complex information spaces.

Use when:

- Location and relationship are part of understanding.

Potential signature:

- A navigable structure diagram, timeline, table topology, or hierarchical map.

### Object-centered

The interface revolves around one recognizable product object: a document, forecast point, booking, person, asset, experiment, or project.

Use when:

- Users think in domain objects rather than application sections.

Potential signature:

- A strong object header, state history, contextual actions, or object-specific visualization.

## Signature selection

A signature is one controlled, repeatable device that makes the product recognizable. It must satisfy all of these:

- Derive from domain material, data, workflow, or culture.
- Improve orientation, comprehension, trust, or interaction.
- Work with real content and edge cases.
- Survive responsive layouts.
- Remain accessible with reduced motion and assistive technology.
- Avoid requiring every component to become decorative.

Good signature sources:

- The geometry of the product's data.
- A recurring physical material or instrument.
- A distinctive temporal or spatial relationship.
- A recognizable document or object model.
- A carefully chosen typographic behavior.
- One meaningful transition between states.

Weak signatures:

- A random gradient.
- Identical rounded icon tiles.
- A repeated glass effect.
- A giant italic word unrelated to the product.
- Floating decorative blobs.
- A stock bento layout with no information logic.

## Density and rhythm

Choose density from task frequency and expertise:

- High-frequency expert work: compact controls, stable alignment, low motion, explicit state.
- Infrequent consumer tasks: more guidance, larger targets, more contextual explanation.
- Reading: controlled measure, quiet intervals, clear navigation.
- Marketing: varied rhythm that follows the argument, not arbitrary section alternation.

Use spacing to communicate hierarchy before adding lines, backgrounds, or cards. Preserve dense professional layouts when they remain usable; spaciousness is not automatically better.

## Responsive translation

Do not treat mobile as a vertical dump of desktop sections.

For each major region, decide whether it:

- stays fixed,
- reorders,
- collapses,
- becomes a disclosure,
- becomes a different control,
- or disappears because it is genuinely secondary.

Preserve reading order and primary action. Test long localized strings, 200% zoom, browser chrome, virtual keyboards, and safe areas.

## Rejection rules

Reject a candidate when:

- It depends on invented content or claims.
- It requires deleting product behavior to make the layout clean.
- It cannot represent loading, empty, error, and dense states.
- It works only at one viewport.
- It copies the recognizable expression of a reference rather than transferring principles.
- It adds a second styling system without a migration plan.
- It makes a professional tool slower to scan.
- Its signature is merely a current design trend.
